Assessment Comparison

Patient: 33-year-old male Assessment 1: 5 September 2025 · Assessment 2: 5 May 2026 (242 days later)

The main takeaway

How this patient feels and how he performs both improved — but the EEG moved the other way. General wellbeing (GHQ-12) improved substantially, depression symptoms (PHQ-9) eased, and objective attention testing got clearly faster and more consistent. Against that, anxiety (GAD-7) rose and stayed above the reference range, and the EEG shifted broadly toward slower activity, with theta/beta ratios moving further outside normal limits. The behavioural and EEG findings point in opposite directions, so the EEG change should be interpreted cautiously rather than taken at face value.

GHQ-12 is the standout. At the first visit most items were answered "same as usual", with strain "rather more than usual" and happiness "less than usual". PHQ-9 improved modestly but unevenly. Low mood, loss of interest, self-criticism and appetite all eased. Two items moved the other way: fatigue worsened ("several days" → "more than half the days") and concentration difficulty appeared ("not at all" → "several days"). GAD-7 is the one clinical signal that worsened. The increase is concentrated in worry and relaxation: "worrying too much about different things" moved from "not at all" to "more than half the days", and "trouble relaxing" from "several days" to "more than half the days". ASRS is essentially unchanged (one item, dependence on others for organisation, moved from "rarely" to "sometimes"). Difficulty unwinding was "often" at both visits, echoing the GAD-7 picture.

Two things are worth noting alongside the out-of-range values. Observed changes versus possible explanations

What is observed is described above. What it means is less certain, for three reasons.

  • The EEG shift is broad and one-directional. Theta and slow-wave activity rose on all six shared channels in both eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions, while beta and high beta fell on all six in both conditions; within the alpha range, low alpha rose and high alpha fell. (Delta was the exception, falling rather than rising.) A shift that broad fits a whole-recording factor just as well — headband fit or electrode contact, drowsiness or tiredness on the day, time of day, caffeine, sleep the night before — as it fits a genuine change in brain function. The reference material specifically links high theta and low beta to fatigue, and the patient independently reported worse fatigue on the PHQ-9.
  • The EEG and the performance data disagree. Higher theta/beta ratios are associated with difficulty focusing, yet the CPT — a direct behavioural measure of sustained attention — improved on speed, consistency and errors, and the ADHD screen was unchanged. When an objective performance measure and a resting EEG marker point in opposite directions, the performance measure is the more direct evidence about attention.
  • These are two single recordings. There is no third data point and no information here about test conditions, sleep, medication or caffeine at either session, so day-to-day variability cannot be separated from a real trend. The z-scores are also drawn from a different channel set at the second visit, though the six shared channels were used for comparison.

None of this rules out a real neurophysiological change — it means one recording pair cannot establish it.

Overall verdict

Clinically, this is a mostly positive interval with one symptom to watch and one measurement question to resolve. General mental health improved markedly and depressive symptoms and their functional impact eased, while objective attention performance improved on every measure. Anxiety moved in the wrong direction and remains above its reference range, driven by worry and difficulty relaxing — the same difficulty-unwinding theme flagged on the ASRS at both visits. The EEG shifted toward slower activity with several theta/beta measures now out of range, but that shift is broad and consistent in direction across most bands and channels, is contradicted by the performance data, and is plausibly influenced by fatigue or recording conditions, so it should not be read as a confirmed change on its own.

Next steps to discuss

  1. Review the anxiety change directly with the patient. Worry and trouble relaxing rose while other symptoms improved — clarify what changed in the last few months (stressors, sleep, work, caffeine) and whether the GAD-7 score matches his own sense of things.
  2. Ask about fatigue and sleep. Fatigue worsened on the PHQ-9, new concentration difficulty appeared, and and the reference material links raised theta to fatigue and the raised low-alpha/high-alpha ratio to sleep difficulties. This is the single thread that could tie the questionnaire and EEG findings together.
  3. Check the recording conditions for the second EEG — headband fit and signal quality, time of day, sleep the previous night, caffeine, and alertness during the session — before treating the slow-wave shift as a real change.
  4. Consider a repeat EEG under controlled, matched conditions (same time of day, same channel set, rested and caffeine-consistent) to establish whether the theta/beta elevation reproduces. A single follow-up recording cannot distinguish a trend from day-to-day variability.
  5. Confirm the change in peak alpha frequency (9 Hz → 8 Hz) on that repeat recording, since it aligns with the low-alpha/high-alpha shift and is the most internally consistent EEG finding.
  6. Continue tracking all four questionnaires and the CPT at the next review, so the divergence between subjective, behavioural and EEG measures can be re-examined with a third data point.
