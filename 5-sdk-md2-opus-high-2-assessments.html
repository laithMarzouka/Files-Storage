What was observed, and what could explain it

The left column is what the numbers say. The right column is what could produce those numbers — none of it established by this data.
Observed
  • Wellbeing improved (GHQ-12 11 → 4) and the depression score improved (PHQ-9 9 → 7). Anxiety rose (GAD-7 11 → 13). The ADHD screen was flat (10 → 11).
  • On the attention task he was faster (443.5 → 362.8 ms) and steadier (54.3 → 34.8 ms), with accuracy 99.5% → 99.75% and errors already at the floor.
  • In the resting EEG, theta rose and high alpha, beta and high beta fell at all six sites recorded on both days, with eyes open and with eyes closed. Low alpha rose at almost all of them.
  • Every slow-to-fast ratio check rose and peak alpha sits at the bottom of its scale, while three of the four reactivity checks were unchanged and normal and the fourth slipped just past its line.
Possible explanations
  • One session, one state. A resting EEG captures a few minutes of one day. Sleep the night before, time of day, caffeine and simple drowsiness all move activity between the 4–9 Hz range and faster bands. None of that is recorded alongside these numbers.
  • The recording differed. The follow-up used eight sensor sites rather than six, and differences in fit and contact change how much of each rhythm a site picks up.
  • Practice. The attention task was one he had already completed once; repeat exposure can improve speed and consistency on its own.
  • A real change is also possible. Nothing here rules out a genuine shift in his resting rhythm. The point is that this data cannot separate the two.
  • What is missing. Signal-quality figures for either session are not included in this comparison, and where a site cannot be recorded cleanly its value is reconstructed from the other sites for that band. Treat the overall pattern as much stronger evidence than any single site.
One thing that does support the comparison: both assessments are scored against the same reference norms for his age and sex, so the standard scores can be read against each other.

Overall verdict

He is doing better on what he reports and on what he performs; one measurement moved the other way. Wellbeing and mood scores improved, and attention-task performance improved on both speed and consistency. Anxiety scores rose. The resting EEG moved in a single, internally consistent direction — more 4–9 Hz activity, less fast activity, a slower dominant rhythm and higher theta/beta ratios everywhere — while the checks that test how his brain responds to closing his eyes and to a task stayed essentially unchanged, with one slipping just past its line.

That EEG pattern is large and coherent, but it rests on one recording session, and it is also the pattern a tired or drowsy resting recording produces. The rise in anxiety and the two questionnaire items about energy and concentration are consistent with that, but this data cannot establish a cause. Nothing here supports a diagnosis, and no conclusion about treatment follows from it. The sensible next move is clarification, not interpretation.
