Assessment Comparison — 8 Months Apart

Assessment 1: 2025-09-05 · Assessment 2: 2026-05-05 (242 days later) · Male, 33

Main takeaway

Two of the three data streams moved in a favourable direction: general wellbeing improved substantially and attention task performance improved on every measure. Anxiety symptoms, however, went up, and the EEG recording shows a large, whole-head shift toward slower activity. The EEG change is big enough that recording conditions (e.g. drowsiness or signal quality) need to be ruled out before it is read as a change in the patient — especially because it points in the opposite direction to the improved task performance.

EEG

Read this section with caution. Assessment 2 included two channels (Fp2 and Tp9) that Assessment 1 did not, so only the six shared sites (Cz, F3, F4, Fp1, Fz, Tp10) can be compared directly. More importantly, the pattern of change at Assessment 2 — theta and low-alpha elevated across nearly the whole head at the same time as beta, low beta and high beta are suppressed across nearly the whole head, plus a drop in peak alpha frequency — is the signature that global drowsiness or a change in signal quality typically produces. It is not possible to separate those explanations from a genuine change in resting brain activity using these data alone.

What the shared channels show, at eyes-open:

  • Theta rose markedly. Frontal theta went from mildly elevated (F3 1.06, F4 1.77, Fz 1.29) to strongly elevated (F3 4.86, F4 4.21, Fz 4.09); central theta rose from 1.16 to 2.67. High eyes-open theta is associated with daydreaming, inattention, brain fog or fatigue.
  • Low alpha (8–9 Hz) rose sharply, from roughly z 0.2–1.5 to z 1.2–4.1 at shared sites, with the new Tp9 channel at z 7.8 — an extreme value that itself warrants a data-quality check.
  • Beta bands dropped well below the reference population. Central beta moved from −1.04 to −2.20, and low beta from −0.62 to −2.44. Low beta can reflect calmness, but also fatigue or reduced attention.
  • High alpha (11–12 Hz) fell, e.g. Cz from 0.97 to −1.88, consistent with the overall slowing.

Overall verdict

Subjectively and behaviourally, this patient is doing better than eight months ago: general health complaints dropped substantially, depressive symptoms and their functional impact eased, and attention-task performance improved in speed, consistency and error rate. Two findings run counter to that: anxiety symptoms increased and remain above the reference range, with difficulty relaxing and irritability persisting; and the EEG shows a pronounced whole-head slowing with suppressed fast activity, alongside newly reported fatigue and concentration difficulty on the PHQ-9.

The discordance is the central interpretive question. Improved reaction time and reduced variability are hard to reconcile with an EEG picture suggesting drowsiness and reduced alertness — which is why the recording conditions should be clarified before the EEG change is treated as a stable finding.

Suggested next steps (for clarification and discussion, not treatment change)

  • Verify the EEG recording itself: check signal quality, impedance and artefact logs for Assessment 2, particularly the extreme Tp9 low-alpha value (z 7.8) and whether Fp2 and Tp9 were newly added or repositioned. Confirm time of day, sleep the night before, caffeine, medication and any observed drowsiness during the session.
  • Consider a repeat EEG under matched conditions (same time of day, same channel set, alertness confirmed) before drawing conclusions from the theta and theta/beta changes.
  • Discuss the anxiety trend directly, focusing on the specific items that worsened: generalised worry across different things, and trouble relaxing. The difficulty-unwinding item is elevated on both the GAD-7 and the ASRS.
  • Ask about fatigue, sleep and concentration, which are newly or increasingly endorsed on the PHQ-9 and are listed in the reference material as factors that can affect peak alpha frequency, the low-alpha/high-alpha ratio and the beta response while counting.
  • Note the practice effect when interpreting the CPT gain; if a further comparison is planned, allow for familiarity with the task.
  • Track the GHQ-12 improvement at the next review to confirm it is sustained rather than reflecting a good period at the time of completion.
