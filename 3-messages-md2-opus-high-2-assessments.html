Main takeaway

Two of three things moved in a good direction: general wellbeing improved substantially, and attention-task performance got faster and far more consistent. Against that, self-reported anxiety rose, and the EEG showed a broad, whole-head shift toward slower brainwave activity that pushed several focus-related ratios out of their normal ranges. How to read this, carefully. Per the reference material, high theta with eyes open is associated with daydreaming, inattention, brain fog or fatigue; a lower peak alpha frequency and reduced fast activity can reflect tiredness. That is one plausible reading, and it is consistent with the patient's worsened fatigue and newly reported concentration difficulty on the PHQ-9. However, three things argue for caution before concluding that attention capacity has declined:

  • The change is uniform across every channel and band — a signature that often reflects overall recording state (drowsiness, time of day, sleep the night before, caffeine) or signal-quality/headband-contact differences, rather than a regional neurophysiological change.
  • The CPT contradicts it. Objective sustained attention got faster and substantially more consistent over the same interval.
  • The channel set differs between visits. Assessment 2 adds Fp2 and Tp9, which have no baseline comparison. Tp9 low alpha reached z = 7.8 (eyes open) and z = 5.3 (eyes closed) — extreme values that warrant a data-quality check before being interpreted at all.

Overall verdict

Subjectively and behaviourally, this is a better picture than eight months ago: general wellbeing improved markedly, depressive symptoms and their functional impact eased, and attention-task performance improved on both speed and consistency. Two findings stand out for attention: anxiety has increased and sits above the "good" range, centred on generalised worry, irritability and an inability to relax; and the EEG shifted broadly toward slower activity, with several focus- and arousal-related ratios now outside their normal ranges. Given the contradiction with the CPT and the global nature of the EEG change, its cause is genuinely uncertain and should not be read as a confirmed change in brain function.

Suggested next steps (clarification, not treatment change)

  • Review EEG recording conditions for Assessment 2: sleep the previous night, time of day, caffeine, alertness during the recording, headband fit and signal quality — especially for the Tp9 channel, whose values are extreme enough to question.
  • Consider a repeat EEG under well-rested, standardised conditions, using the same channel set as baseline, to establish whether the slowing is stable or state-dependent.
  • Discuss the anxiety picture in session: what has changed in the last two weeks to broaden the worry, what "trouble relaxing" looks like day to day, and how it interacts with the persistent "difficulty unwinding" reported on the ASRS at both visits.
  • Explore the fatigue and new concentration complaint, which are the two questionnaire items that worsened and the ones most likely to connect the subjective report to the EEG finding; screen for sleep, workload and medical contributors as clinically appropriate.
  • Note the divergence between report and performance: the patient notices more difficulty concentrating while the objective task improved. Clarifying where the difficulty shows up (long tasks, reading, meetings) will indicate whether further targeted assessment is useful.
